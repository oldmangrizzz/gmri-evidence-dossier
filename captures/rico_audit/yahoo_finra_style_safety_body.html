Three Frontier Labs Are Building a FINRA-Style Safety Body. History Suggests It Won’t Be a Brake.

Lena Park
September 19, 2026 3 min read
  • GOOG +5.34%

September 15, 2026, marked a shift in AI governance when OpenAI Chief Global Affairs Officer Chris Lehane confirmed during a Washington briefing that OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind have been coordinating on safety protocols for several weeks. This confirmation moves the industry from abstract discussion toward active coalition-building, though the proposed structure remains unformalized. While OpenAI has been vocal, Anthropic and Google have not publicly confirmed this specific coordination to reporters, leaving the initiative in a state of strategic ambiguity.

The Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) provided a framework that allowed for rapid market expansion under the guise of oversight, effectively lowering political friction while entrenching existing players. By adopting this model, first floated by Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis on July 14, the three labs aim to create an industry-funded, federally overseen entity that reviews models up to 30 days before release. This approach functions less as a brake on development and more as a mechanism to secure the social license required to continue scaling without the threat of restrictive, external government intervention.

Meta, xAI, and NVIDIA openly opposed new government-led regulation at Dreamforce on September 15, highlighting that the proposed body is a strategic bloc rather than an industry-wide standard. This fracture underscores that the coalition is one of convenience. Even within the group, alignment is tenuous, as the industry continues to grapple with the accountability infrastructure designed to preemptively shape the regulatory environment.

Seven days after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a 3,800-word essay on September 12 calling for an industry-wide slowdown, Reuters reported that the company is considering a new model release to counter GPT-6 Astra. This gap between pacing rhetoric and competitive reality suggests that safety is being treated as an operating model rather than a hard stop, a theme explored in our analysis of the Amodei Pacing Framework. As detailed in our coverage of the Anthropic IPO, the company must satisfy investors hungry for growth while simultaneously convincing regulators that they are the primary stewards of safety.

Cohere CEO Aidan Gomez has labeled the proposal a cartel by any other name, drawing direct parallels to the SEC's 1975 NRSRO designation and Europe's 1985 Motor Vehicle Block Exemption. The argument holds that such a body would inevitably entrench incumbents, locking in current advantages while creating high barriers to entry for smaller competitors and open-source developers. This critique highlights the tension inherent in the industry's attempt to become the architects of their own oversight.

The FRONTIER Act (H.R.9925), introduced in July by Representatives Obernolte and Trahan, proposes an alternative path: independent verification organizations (IVOs) licensed by NIST/CAISI to assess developers every six months. This legislative approach contrasts sharply with the labs' preference for industry-led standards, which OpenAI's Lehane noted should be pursued with or without government support. Meanwhile, White House AI czar David Sacks has characterized the industry's self-regulatory proposals as potential regulatory capture or an election-season distraction.

Capital market pressures create a persistent friction between the safety thesis and the growth thesis. Labs are attempting to resolve this tension by building a self-regulatory body that allows them to manage their own oversight. This maneuvering highlights the contradictions inherent in business models that must satisfy investors while navigating a complex regulatory landscape.

Can this coalition survive the competing interests of its members and the scrutiny of the Trump administration? While Mark Zuckerberg reportedly told the President that the FINRA model was flawed, the administration has not abandoned the concept, with officials like Treasury Secretary Bessent and Chief of Staff Wiles keeping the proposal under review. For now, the industry operates in a gray zone, coordinating on safety and preparing for a legislative landscape that may ultimately bypass their preferred, industry-led solutions.
