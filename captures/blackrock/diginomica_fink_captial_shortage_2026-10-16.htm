Paying the AI piper (1/2 - how are we going to keep the funding plates spinning when the capital is running out? BlackRock CEO Larry Fink is worried

By Stuart Lauchlan October 16, 2026
Summary:
The interdependent 'circular funding' going on in the AI market is problematic enough without there being a shortage of ready capital looming...
  • Facebook
  • Linkedin
  • Reddit
  • Twitter
  • Threads
  • Bluesky


For the first time in my career, and that's 50 years of a career, I'm seeing genuine shortages of capital.

That’s an interesting - and alarming - remark when it comes from Larry Fink, founder and CEO of BlackRock, the world’s largest asset management firm.

It follows this week’s warning from the International Monetary Fund (IMF) that the current “circular financing” going on in the AI supplier sector poses a genuine risk to the economy if it breaks down.

And that seems all-too-possible as Fink goes on to comment:

I don't see us having the ability to raise trillions and trillions of dollars of bespoke transactions related to data centers.

But someone’s going to have to find the cash as Fink has also warned this week that stopping or even slowing down the build out of AI infrastructure could tip the US into recession and sink the economy. And when America catches a cold, the rest of the world sneezes...

Precedents

Is this just more armageddon pedling? Fears around an AI version of the East India Company economic bubble collapse of the 18th century have circulated for a long time now, but the plates are still spinning for the moment. But, of course, in politics and economics, things can change in an instant.

We have been here before though, Fink argues, and we don’t need to go back to the 18th century to see it:

In the early 1980s, mortgages were loans sitting on banks' balance sheets, illiquid and hard to price. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac began pooling them, but that alone didn't make them tradable. What made them tradable was data, years of performance history and understanding of how the performance would move with interest rates and with the changes of the broader economy.

Once investors had a trusted time series of data, issuers had a standard to build against and the market opened up for a much bigger group of investors. That combination took the MBS market from a few billion dollars when I started to the trillions of dollars it is today.

What does that have to do with today’s AI hype frenzy? He goes on:

Compute capacity is now following a similar path, and we intend at BlackRock to be the center of it as we were then in the beginnings of the mortgage-backed securities market....As compute capacity emerges as an asset class, the markets will need trusted data, standardized performance metrics and a common framework for risk analytics.

It’s not a propitious time, suggests Fink:

Governments are running historically large deficits, while at the same time, demand for private capital is rising across the economy. Governments need financing for infrastructure. Companies need to fund a generational AI build-out...Everyone is re-thinking how they deploy capital.

Opportunity?

BlackRock has a role to play here, he says:

We're both a trusted adviser and a destination for global client capital. That's driving meaningful activity across the firm and a growing pipeline for the future. This is especially visible through our work with the leading companies at the center of the AI build-out.

We have long-standing relationships with these hyperscalers and sovereign wealth funds and pension funds and corporate clients around the world. These long-term relationships are now underpinning some of the largest financing engagements in the market.

Fink cites the example of his firm’s work with Meta on financing its planned $14 billion data center in Texas. It’s just one exemplar of what can be done, he adds:

We’re also working alongside leading technology companies to create dedicated pools of financing for their customers to expand compute capabilities and capacity.  In the near term, these can supply private placements into high-grade insurance mandates.

In the past week, Fink reckons to have had conversations with every major hyperscaler:

It is a large opportunity, both in the financing of GPUs [and] it is a large opportunity for our GIP (Global Infrastructure Provider) team in terms of the build-out and working alongside hyperscalers alongside for data centers.

Earlier this year, BlackRock took part in the $$0 billion acquisition of Aligned Data Centers, a global data center developer. This may not be the last skin in the game that the firm looks to secure, says Fink:

We're actually looking at two more platforms throughout the world where we could play a larger, larger role. As one of the largest shareholders of every major public hyperscaler and technology platform, we're having broad-based conversations with all of them. Obviously, many of them are looking not just to be building out in the United States, but building out in other parts of the world as computing could be necessary throughout the world. We're engaged in that. We're going to be a part of that.

Re-invented financing

But while noting that BlackRock is already mobilizing capital at a greater scale than ever before, Fink’s opening comment about not being able to raise the trillions of dollars needed for bespoke transactions related to data centers remains true. He admits that a re-think is needed on a grand scale when it comes to financing such projects:

The need to create a new financing vehicle for compute for chips is imperative for the US to continue to build out to the scale that is necessary. And once we build this out, the entire world is going to be needing to build this out. If you think about a 1 gigawatt data center in the United States is about a $50 billion enterprise cost, 40% of that is the cost of GPUs and chips. The other 60% is power, grid, core and shell, and all of that comes together.

We are now seeing financing for the data centers that are based on the lease of the credit, but now what we are also witnessing the need to build out the scale of chips and compute. If we're going to democratize AI, the faster we could do this, the better we'll be in broadening economic success.

And such a democratization is an economic imperative, he declares, to make sure that too much power doesn’t rest in the hands of a tiny cabal of Tech Bros:

We cannot have just five companies or seven companies entirely buying out every chip production that is being produced right now. We need the ability to finance start-up AI companies, start-up companies, but each one of these companies are going to need the ability to buy these new innovative compute chips.

They don't have a credit rating, obviously, and so we have to come up with innovative ways. The best way is that we could do an asset-based financing related to the chips through data, through analytics, then we can help finance start-up companies, the growth of new and young companies.

This would have knock-on beneficial economic effect, he suggests:

It can transform America to grow at three percent or greater, and then the debt that I just talked about will not be as consequential because we can continue to grow our economy at three-plus percent and our debt-to-GDP should fall.

Clients worldwide are all in need of the same sort of re-imagined financing, he adds:

It really requires having the unique structuring capabilities, working with the hyperscalers, but then working with our clients in terms of what are they looking for, what type of structure are they looking for, what type of guarantees do they need in the back end if it's compute. we're having conversations literally daily on opportunities that we get help out.

If you think about the need in the United States alone between the need of building out our power grid, the need for securing power and the need for the build-out of core and shell, we're talking $7 trillion to $9 trillion over the course of a number of years. Obviously, that is a lot of financing - and that's just in the United States. You have other places in the world that are looking for the same type of financing capabilities, too.

My take

Not exactly cheerful reading - and there’s more to come. In our next article, we look at how the likes of Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs are facing up to the challenge of making sure those plates keep spinning.
