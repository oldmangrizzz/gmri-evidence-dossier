Army Moves Iowa Lead Azide Detonation to Wednesday, Says It Can Proceed Without Permit

Local residents are being told by federal authorities to keep their windows closed for one or two days following the Nov. 25 detonation.

The U.S. Army has again moved the date of its detonation of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant to Wednesday, this time to Wednesday, Nov. 25. The service told a federal appeals court it can proceed without the environmental permit the court has put on hold.

The service said Sunday evening on social media that the change would "allow for optimal weather conditions and all mitigation measures to be emplaced." It gave no time and said that "additional details on this situation will be forthcoming."

Health officials in Iowa and Illinois have advised people in affected areas to stay indoors on Wednesday, the day before Thanksgiving, with windows closed for 24 to 48 hours after the operation.

A joint statement provided to Military.com on behalf of the Army, EPA and Department of Health and Human Services said that all agencies are working together "to ensure that the controlled detonation in Middletown, Iowa, is conducted in the safest fashion possible."

"Officials have been meticulously planning for this event since March, and all agencies involved are prepared to take the most extreme mitigation measures available to ensure there is no risk to the public and any disruptions are minimized," the statement said. "The entire Trump administration will continue to engage with state and local officials, as well as the broader community, to keep all apprised of updates as they arise."

Three Times in a Week

Friday, Nov. 20, was the originally scheduled date for the detonation. But one day prior, acting Army Secretary Adam Telle postponed it to Monday.

He called it the "latest possible moment prior to a forecasted temperature drop" that could further destabilize the material. (LINK TO NOV. 20 PIECE)

On Saturday, Justice Department lawyers told the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals that "the Army intends to act on Nov. 24, 2026," according to their filing.

A day later, the Army's post named Wednesday and did not explain the change.

No Court Ruling Yet

On Thursday night, the court acted on a challenge from the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club.

"An administrative stay of the emergency permit issued by the EPA is granted until the emergency motion for stay is ruled upon by this court," the order says.

No ruling on that motion had been reported as of Monday morning.

In its Saturday filing, the government said the Army "intends to invoke the emergency munitions and explosives exclusion" in federal hazardous waste rules.

That order "suspends the authority the Permit conferred, but it does not enjoin or otherwise prohibit the Army from acting under a separate legal authority," the filing says.

On Sunday, the government asked the court to lift the stay, CNN reported.

Are Safeguards in Place?

It was the paused permit that set the Army's obligations after the detonation.

These included an updated plume estimate within six hours, soil and surface sampling beginning within 24 hours, and a final report to the EPA within 30 days.

"The Army intends to act consistent with the terms of the Emergency Permit and its mitigation measures even if not in effect," the filing says. That is a statement of intent.

Whether those terms could be enforced if the Army acts under the exclusion is not addressed in the filing.

Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds said Sunday that the state's natural resources staff "will independently evaluate all monitoring data." Her announcement does not say the state will collect samples of its own.

A Sworn Declaration, and the 'Only Feasible Option'

A declaration attached to the filing provides the most complete public account of the stockpile.

Col. Aaron Teller, who commands the Army's 71st Ordnance Group, said the 68 drums hold 9,773.5 pounds of material, and that an April 28 inspection found deterioration in 58 of them.

He also said the plant's operating contractor, American Ordnance, had not checked the liquid the material is stored in since September 2025. Preparations for the operation have left the storage bunkers more exposed to outside temperatures.

On Sept. 9, 2025, Army Contracting Command posted a notice titled "Lead Azide Neutralization at Iowa Army Ammunition Plant," federal procurement records show.

The Army's environmental assessment says the degraded stockpile was identified in March 2026. Nothing in the notice shows what officials knew about its condition in 2025.

"This emergency is of the Army's own making," the groups said in a statement Sunday.

The groups' lawyers no longer argue that detonation can be avoided.

"It appears that detonation may be, at this point, the only feasible option due to how long this problem was allowed to persist," said Michael Schmidt, general counsel for the Iowa Environmental Council.

In a declaration for the groups, retired EPA official Mark Thomas disputed the cold-weather risk, writing that the forecast is "comparable to conditions the stored material has already experienced," KCRG reported.

What Residents Should Know

Army officials have not said whether the 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. window it gave one Middletown neighborhood to leave on the original date applies this Wednesday.

Updated plume maps are supposed to be posted on the Army's website.

Property owners who want their land sampled must sign an Army access form. It grants "an irrevocable and assignable right to enter" for up to 36 months and allows temporary structures and groundwater monitoring wells, with one day's notice before crews arrive.

Damage claims can be filed with the Army for up to two years, according to an Army fact sheet.

As of Monday morning, the permit remained on hold and the Army had not said what time Wednesday the operation will begin.
