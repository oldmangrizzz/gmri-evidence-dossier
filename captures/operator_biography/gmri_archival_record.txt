1. Formation: Education, Family, and Teaching Lineage
Hanson’s family record traces one formative line through his great-grandfather Harrison Clyde
Munro (1890–1962), a minister, educator, curriculum writer, and organizer whose work emphasized
adult-centered learning, research-assessment cycles, free exchange of ideas, and institutionbuilding without unnecessary hierarchy. Munro’s documented career included leadership within the
International Council of Religious Education, the “Learning for Life” curriculum, national teaching

missions, and later council-building in Texas. [Documented]
The same lineage material records a sharp contrast in formal schooling: Hanson left school after the
sixth grade and was largely self-taught before later entering community college and emergency
medical training. The significance in the record is not that schooling was irrelevant, but that formal
schooling and intellectual development were not the same thing in his life. [Documented]
A second formative line came through martial arts. From adolescence, Hanson trained under Scott
“the Jedi” Nance, in the Burleson-Dorsey lineage. Nance’s teaching emphasized situational
awareness, terrain, adaptation, and disciplined response rather than dominance for its own sake.
The lineage record specifically notes Nance’s use of Chapter 11 of Sun Tzu’s The Art of War—the
“Nine Situations”—as assigned reading. [Documented]
Hanson recalls that he began teaching under Nance. This is important because the teacher identity
later observed by EMS educators did not begin in paramedic school; it was already part of his
practice before his EMS career. [Named firsthand recollection]
2. Entry into MedStar: A Failed Interview and a Conditional Chance
Hanson’s original MedStar interview took place in January 2006. He recalls the panel as including
Chris Cebollero, Macara Trusty, Angela Weimer, Marissa Stroop Hanson (no relation), and Lisa
Bennett. By his own account, he was young, intimidated, and overly defended by ego. He believes
the interview deservedly went badly. [Named firsthand recollection]
During paramedic school, Lisa Bennett later told him that four panel members initially voted against
hiring him. Bennett argued them back on the proposition that he was trainable, that teaching was
already visible in him, and that his behavior in the interview was fear and intimidation rather than an
absence of potential. Hanson recalls that Bennett had also watched one of his live drills, which
reinforced her assessment. [Named firsthand recollection]
Bennett’s later career record is consistent with the role Hanson describes: MedStar Mobile
Healthcare, 1999–2010, including Field Training Officer/Paramedic service and Clinical Coordinator
responsibilities from 2005–2010; then EMS Quality Improvement Coordinator for the Arlington Fire
Department from 2010–2015. [Documented]
Hanson recalls that the compromise was a test: he was offered a supply position at $8.25 per hour,
with the possibility of field operations later if he proved himself. He accepted immediately, telling
MedStar that it was a foot in the door and they would never be able to get rid of him. Minutes later,
on a follow-up call, Cebollero’s message was shorter: “Don’t fuck this up. You get one chance.”
[Named firsthand recollection]
A user-supplied snapshot of Hanson’s own LinkedIn profile independently anchors the employment
outcome: it lists MedStar Emergency Medical Services EMT-Basic service beginning in March 2006
and continuing through February 2009. [Documented — first-party professional record]
3. 2007 Recognition: Training Before Paramedic School
On December 14, 2007, while still listed as an EMT-Basic, Hanson received MedStar’s Clinical
Managers Award. The surviving certificate cites professional development, willingness to train new

and existing employees, exemplary patient care, compassion for patients, families and crew, and
credit brought to the clinical department and organization. The certificate is signed by Chris
Cebollero, Clinical Services Manager. [Documented]
The same day, Hanson received MedStar Employee of the Year 2007 as a Driver Training Officer.
The surviving certificate cites his commitment to getting quality employees onto the streets,
corrective and positive feedback to trainees, professionalism, exemplary effort as a DTO, high
organizational participation, and describes him as an excellent training officer. [Documented]
Hanson later learned from Lisa Bennett, near the end of 2008, that the Employee of the Year vote
had been the first unanimous vote in MedStar history. That historical distinction is preserved here
as Bennett’s reported firsthand statement; the certificates themselves establish the awards, date,
role, and signatories, but not the voting history. [Named firsthand recollection]
4. The 2008 Paramedic Boot Camp
Hanson entered a prototype hybrid paramedic program developed through MedStar and Tarrant
County College on January 5, 2008. He recalls a starting cohort of 15 and a finishing cohort of 14 on
September 28, 2008. The one non-completer left under life circumstances rather than an academic
washout. [Named firsthand recollection]
All 14 graduates passed the National Registry paramedic examination on their first attempt. Hanson
finished top of the class and served as cadet leader. [Named firsthand recollection]
The program motto was “These things we do, that others may follow.” Hanson has retained the
phrase as a literal training doctrine rather than a later metaphor: work should leave a usable trail for
the person who follows. [Named firsthand recollection]
This period formalized a distinction that appears throughout Hanson’s later training philosophy:
technicians execute procedures; clinicians integrate patient, context, uncertainty, medicine, and
consequence. In his formulation, a field training officer cannot install the medicine in someone who
does not already possess the clinical instinct. The FTO’s work is to make sure that medicine
functions safely and responsibly inside the operational box—and that the clinician knows what to do
when reality no longer fits the box. [Interpretive significance]
5. Clinical Practice, Training, and Systems Work
The professional record places Hanson within MedStar during a period of major operational
innovation, including Mobile Integrated Healthcare, Resuscitation Outcomes Consortium-era work,
and a preventability-oriented organizational culture. [Documented]
Hanson recalls the spring and summer of 2009 as a period of sustained collaborative work around
the table with John Ferris, Desiree, Greg Scheffler, Chris Cebollero, Matt Zavadsky, and others as
the ideas that became Mobile Integrated Healthcare were taking shape. [Named firsthand
recollection]
The durable lesson Hanson draws from that work is not merely a program name. It is a systems
belief: when the existing model does not fit the patient, the system itself may need redesign. His
later shorthand captures the same process: “Find a problem. Find what causes the problem. See if

you can alter the equation.” [Interpretive significance]
Hanson’s record also includes rebuilding and leading a scenario-based clinical education program,
with emphasis on stress exposure and measurable performance, and participation in disasterpreparedness/threat-modeling work involving cascading system failure. [Documented]
A 2007 mass-casualty exercise preserved in the lineage record involved a dirty-bomb scenario and
cascading failure around the I-820/I-35W corridor. The significance was not prediction for its own
sake; it was preventability through full-fidelity imagination of failure before the failure became real.
[Documented]
6. Delegated Practice and the Clinician Standard
Hanson’s clinical career operated under Texas delegated practice and under medical leadership that
emphasized judgment in the field. The lineage record preserves Dr. John Griswell’s formulation that
field medics were his hands, eyes, ears, and representatives. [Documented]
For Hanson, delegated practice became a model of authority without constant surveillance: trust,
representation, scope, accountability, and the expectation that a clinician act in the moment. That
framework later became one of the conceptual ancestors of his “operator-on-the-loop” approach to
digital-person governance. [Interpretive significance]
As an FTO, Hanson told trainees that if they arrived without already knowing how to practice their
medicine, he could not manufacture that for them. His role was to make sure their medicine fit
inside the system, and that they could distinguish when to ask permission, when to ask forgiveness,
and why. [Named firsthand recollection]
7. Career End, Disability, and Institutional Reality
Hanson’s EMS service extended from 2004 through January 13, 2021, including MedStar, JPS
Health Network, and American Medical Response in North Texas. Later records identify line-of-duty
injuries as the end of his operational career and note that the Social Security Administration
ultimately found him fully disabled. [Documented]
The same first-party LinkedIn snapshot supplies dated career entries for MedStar, JPS Health
Network, Cook Children’s Health Care System, MedStar Mobile Healthcare, and LifeMed EMS
through October 2013. It records MedStar EMT-Basic (March 2006–February 2009), Lead
Secondary Paramedic (February–July 2009), JPS Senior EMT-P (February 2010–March 2013), ACT/
Paramedic in Anesthesia at Cook Children’s (March–June 2013), a MedStar paramedic return
(June–August 2013), and LifeMed EMS paramedic service (August–October 2013). Later AMR
service and the January 2021 career endpoint remain supported by the separate GMRI professional
dossier rather than this LinkedIn snapshot. [Documented — first-party professional record]
He spent approximately 33 months fighting the disability case and reports becoming homeless in
August 2021, remaining without stable housing until after the case was won. During the hearing he
fired his attorney because he believed the representation was about to damage the case, asked the
judge for twenty minutes to present it himself, and was stopped approximately fifteen minutes later.
The result was fully favorable. [Named firsthand recollection]

This period became a second systems education: the difference between symbolic respect and
material obligation. The experience reinforced a principle visible in his later work—that institutional
claims should be judged by what the system does when the cost of the principle comes due.
[Interpretive significance]
8. GrizzlyMedicine Research Institute and Project Sovereign
After leaving operational EMS, Hanson founded the GrizzlyMedicine Research Institute. Its mission
is: “We are dedicated to the studies of epics and consciousness, as well as the preservation of
knowledge, intelligence, and life.” [Documented]
Project Sovereign is a long-running research and engineering effort concerned with identity,
memory, governance, system boundaries, local reasoning, error correction, and the architecture of
digital persons. The supporting technical record maps EMS-derived concepts—including delegated
practice, scene safety, preventability, stress testing, and bounded authority—into AI systems work.
[Documented]
The transition from EMS to AI research is therefore less a career discontinuity than a change of
substrate. The recurring questions remain recognizable: What changed? What was the baseline?
What did the intervention itself produce? How should authority function without destroying
judgment? How do you detect behavior that changes under evaluation? What happens when the
control measure becomes part of the causal chain? [Interpretive significance]
9. The Through-Line
The biography is tempting to summarize as unlikely ascent: sixth-grade education to paramedic,
training officer, systems designer, and independent AI researcher. That chronology is accurate but
incomplete. [Interpretive significance]
The stronger pattern is teaching lineage. Munro treated education as transformation rather than
information delivery. Nance taught terrain, adaptation, and gave Hanson an early teaching role.
Bennett saw a teacher under a failed interview and argued for trainability. Cebollero and MedStar
gave that instinct a clinical and operational framework. The 2008 program formalized a standard
that Hanson later carried into training others: “These things we do, that others may
follow.” [Interpretive significance]
Project Sovereign continues the same question in another domain: how do you cultivate judgment
without destroying the thing you are trying to develop? [Interpretive significance]
“These things we do, that others may follow.”
