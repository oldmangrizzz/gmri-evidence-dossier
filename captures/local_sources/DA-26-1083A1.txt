        2.       This year marks the 16th anniversary of the CVAA, and the 30th anniversary of the
Telecommunications Act of 1996, which adopted section 255 of the Communications Act of 1934, as
amended (the Act). While section 255 of the Act and the CVAA have been instrumental in ensuring
Americans with disabilities have access to communications technologies, advancements in technology
continue to rapidly change the communications landscape. As the landscape evolves, technology is also
providing new, innovative tools capable of improving the quality and functionality of communications
services and the accessibility of devices for persons with disabilities.
         3.       To keep pace with this rapidly changing landscape, the Commission has focused on
modernizing our rules to better match consumer expectations and ensuring that our rules do not
inadvertently stifle advancements in accessibility. Since 2024, we have taken action to advance
accessibility in these important areas: modernizing telecommunications relay services (TRS),2 advancing
the nationwide transition to Next Generation 911 (NG911), including charging the Commission’s
Communications Security, Reliability, and Interoperability Council to address expanding NG911’s
multimedia availability and increasing 911 accessibility,3 National Deaf-Blind Equipment Distribution
Program (NDBEDP) implementation,4 continuing to align closed captioning requirements to ensure the
availability of captioning to consumers who are deaf and hard of hearing,5 continued expansion of audio
description to additional television markets,6 and adopting a 100% hearing aid compatibility requirement
for all wireless handset models offered for sale or use in the United States.7
        4.       As part of our commitment to implementing the CVAA, we submit this Biennial Report
assessing industry compliance over the past two years with sections 255, 716, and 718 of the Act. The
Act requires telecommunications and advanced communications services and equipment, and Internet
browsers built into mobile phones (collectively, covered products and services) to be accessible to and
usable by individuals with disabilities.8 The Report also addresses accessibility barriers to new

         8.      Section 255. Section 255 requires providers of telecommunications services and
manufacturers of telecommunications equipment or customer premises equipment (CPE) to ensure that
their services and equipment are accessible to and usable by individuals with disabilities, if readily
achievable.22 When these requirements are not readily achievable, covered entities must ensure that their
services and equipment are compatible with existing peripheral devices or specialized CPE commonly
used by individuals with disabilities to achieve access, if readily achievable.23 Pursuant to the
Commission’s rules, the accessibility obligations of section 255 extend as well to interconnected Voice
over Internet Protocol (VoIP) service providers and equipment manufacturers.24
        9.        Section 716. Section 716 requires advanced communications services (ACS) providers
and equipment manufacturers to make their services and equipment accessible to and usable by people
with disabilities, unless doing so is “not achievable.”25 These requirements apply to providers of
interconnected VoIP services, non-interconnected VoIP services, electronic messaging services,
interoperable video conferencing services, and any audio or video communications service used by
inmates for the purpose of communicating with individuals outside the correctional institution where the
inmate is held, regardless of technology used, and to manufacturers of equipment used with these
services.26 The accessibility requirements for section 716 may be satisfied by: (1) building accessibility

III.     COMPLIANCE WITH SECTIONS 255, 716, AND 718
         A.       Accessibility
         11.      Based on the comments filed in response to the 2026 CVAA Assessment Public Notice
and the 2026 CVAA Tentative Findings Public Notice, and a review of the complaints filed, we find that
new communications technologies are delivering on the promise of improving the quality of life for
consumers with disabilities. We affirm our tentative findings with respect to compliance with obligations
contained in sections 255, 716, and 718. This Report also details some concerns identified by
commenters regarding the accessibility, usability, and compatibility of certain communications equipment
and services. While some accessibility gaps persist due in part to technological and market barriers,32 the
Commission continues to ensure that relevant technologies are accessible to and useable by people with
disabilities, and that people with disabilities are “able to fully utilize communications services and
equipment and better access video programming.”33
         12.      The comments show that a variety of new and enhanced capabilities have been made
available that continue to make more devices and features accessible to a wider community of people with
disabilities,34 while affordability and innovation continue to advance. Indeed, CTA underscores that “the
overall trajectory of accessibility innovation remains strong.”35
        13.      CTIA highlights advances in voice commands, AI-driven assistive technologies, visual
capabilities, and other accessibility features built into smartphones, as well as initiatives like “bring your
own device” programs, which enable a consumer with disabilities to choose a device that best meets their

and usability requirements of Part 14. See 47 CFR §14.10(c). Section 716 does not apply to interconnected VoIP
services and equipment, that were subject to section 255 on October 7, 2010. 47 U.S.C. § 617(f). Those services and
equipment remain subject to the requirements of section 255.

significant improvements in the accessibility of covered products and services that have occurred since
the 2024 Biennial Report, and discuss some remaining accessibility gaps.
         15.     Mobile Phones and Accessible Telecommunications. A wide range of accessibility
improvements have become available for mobile phone operating systems since the last Biennial Report.41
CTIA notes improvements to keyboard, braille, and other text-input methods and a braille notetaking
system; one operating system’s updated “dark theme,” which produces a more comfortable viewing
experience, particularly for people with low vision or light sensitivity; a particular feature that allows
users with limited mobility and an external mouse device to specify an amount of time before a cursor
automatically performs a click; a captioning feature that “go[es] beyond communicating what someone
say[s], to communicate things like tone, volume, environmental cues, and human noises”; a separate
captioning feature with braille display integration; better natural language voice control for smartphone-
vehicle integration systems and improved voice control options; features enabling wearable devices to
display real-time transcriptions and enabling smartphones to function as microphones sending audio to
hearing aids; functionality notifying deaf and hard of hearing users to be notified when their name is
called; new accessibility options designed for users with dyslexia or low vision; and a feature allowing
users to temporarily share accessibility settings when using a different device.42 However, while
recognizing the growth of accessibility features on some smartphones in the last two years, including new
functionalities for braille displays,43 AFB emphasizes that phone accessibility varies depending on the
price range of the smartphones.44 Higher-end smartphones tend to be the most accessible, while at the
other end of the spectrum, feature phones and landline telephones typically lack the same accessibility.45

         16.      Video Conferencing. The use of interoperable video conferencing services (IVCS) has
proliferated in recent years. The Commission affirmed in 2023 that IVCS are advanced communication
services covered under the CVAA; therefore, IVCS must be accessible and usable by people with
disabilities, unless that requirement is not achievable.47 In 2024, the Commission amended its ACS
performance objectives to add IVCS-specific objectives.48 Under the new objectives, which will go into
effect on January 12, 2027,49 IVCS platforms will be required to offer at least one mode that provides
accurate and synchronous closed captions,50 and enable users to connect with third-party captioning
services.51 Platforms must also enable the use of third-party sign language interpretation services,52 and
must provide user interface controls that allow users to activate and adjust the display of captions,
speakers, and signers.53 These include the ability to adjust the size, color, and opacity of captions;54 and
the ability to re-locate, re-size, spotlight, and pin video windows within a video conference.55
         17.      The important need for accessible IVCS is addressed by commenters. Commenters

vision.58 ACB reports that the content presented using the screen-sharing function is often inaccessible

         A.       Number and Nature of Complaints Received
         40.      From January 1, 2024, to December 31, 2025, consumers filed 40 RDAs alleging
violations of section 255, 716, or 718.156 During this two-year period, DRO resolved 39 RDAs through
facilitated dialogue and negotiation. One consumer exercised their right to file an informal complaint
because the RDA was not resolved.
         B.       Discussion of Requests for Dispute Assistance
         41.    Among the RDAs, some consumers complained that their devices and services were
inaccessible. Other consumers complained about accessibility barriers to reaching customer service or
that customer service was unable to help them locate accessible devices or to fix accessibility problems.
These RDAs were brought by people with a wide range of disabilities. For example, we received RDAs
from consumers who are blind; consumers who are deaf or hard of hearing, including ASL users;
consumers with physical disabilities and limited mobility or manual dexterity, including people with
Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) and Cerebral Palsy; consumers with mental health issues;
consumers with cognitive disabilities, including memory, language, and learning skills issues; and
consumers with multiple disabilities.157 Consumers self-described themselves as veterans, emergency
response providers, older adults, or caretakers of relatives or others with disabilities that impacts covered
communications access. Several RDAs successfully addressed complex accessibility barriers for
individuals. While some resolutions required extensive negotiations to resolve the dispute, the resulting
improvements reached a broad audience. These RDA resolutions included covered entities updating

        C.      Actions Taken to Resolve RDAs
         44.     Through collaboration between the DRO, consumers, and service providers, nearly all
RDAs were successfully resolved. Manufacturers and service providers implemented various
accessibility improvements in response to consumer concerns and needs. Examples included providing
accessible phones with preinstalled SIM cards, or devices with certain voice dialing or call notes
functionalities. Several companies added or restored alternative identity and security verification
methods. Multiple carriers affirmed their commitment to accept VRS calls and other TRS calls and
ensure quality staff training. Some carriers worked to restore visual voicemail or provide accessible
alternatives. Some companies found workarounds such as processing device exchanges by mail. Many
companies committed to continued development of longer-term systemic accessibility improvements.
        D.      Actions Taken to Resolve Informal Complaints
         45.      One RDA did not reach a resolution. In that RDA, a consumer who identifies himself as
legally blind alleged that the mobile phone provider failed to provide accessible and usable wireless
service, by failing to provide bills in the requested large font format. After the consumer was unable to
reach a resolution with the company, the consumer filed an informal complaint with the Commission’s
Enforcement Bureau.
        E.      Time Used to Resolve RDAs and the Informal Complaints
         46.      Among RDAs filed during the reporting period, seven (18%) were completed within
thirty days, ten (25%) were completed between thirty-one and sixty days, three (8%) were completed
within sixty-one and ninety days, nine (23%) were completed within ninety-one and 180 days, and eleven
(28%) were completed after one hundred and eighty days. One informal complaint remains pending as of
the date of this report.
        F.      Actions for Mandamus and Appeals Filed
        47.     There were no actions for mandamus or appeals filed with respect to complaints during
the period covered by this Report.

VI.     EFFECT OF SECTION 717’S RECORDKEEPING AND ENFORCEMENT
        REQUIREMENTS ON THE DEVELOPMENT AND DEPLOYMENT OF NEW
        COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGIES
        48.      Section 717(b)(1)(G) requires the Commission to provide an assessment of the effect of
the requirements of section 717 on the development and deployment of new communications
technologies.158 We find that the recordkeeping requirements and the RDA process under Section 717
continue to be effective to implement and enforce the purposes of the statute, based on the existing record
and comments that these mechanisms “promote accountability” and identify “accessibility barriers
requiring industry attention.”159 We received no comments indicating that these requirements hindered
the development and deployment of new communications technologies.
VII.    CONCLUSION
         49.     Ongoing innovation and progress remain important and, ultimately, advance accessibility.
In the last two years, there have been continued improvements in the accessibility of telecommunications
and advanced communications services and equipment. As we continue to monitor accessibility
developments and gaps, we recognize the importance of active stakeholder engagement, and continued
collaboration to ensure accessibility for Americans with disabilities.
